AI terms, plainly · No. 1

Embedding

A customer asks "can I get my money back?" The document says "refund." Not one word matches. So how does a computer know those two mean the same thing? That's what this page is about.

In one line: an embedding is the step that turns things into "meaning coordinates". Once you have coordinates, you can find things by meaning, recommend and spot anomalies. Model makers sell it, databases store it, and software companies build it into their products.

2
Then the problem · where things get stuck without it

Matching words finds nothing

The simplest approach is keyword search. But the customer said "money back" and the document says "refund". Not a single word matches, so nothing comes up.

So the computer needs a new skill: telling whether two sentences mean the same thing.

3
How it works · one picture that explains it

A map of meaning

Computers don't understand meaning, but they're great with numbers. An embedding turns each sentence into a list of numbers, like coordinates on a map:

"money back" → [0.12, -0.83, 0.41, …]

Sentences that mean similar things land close together: "refund", "money back" and "I want to return this" sit in one corner, while "how long is shipping?" lands far away in another. "Do these mean the same?" becomes "how close are these two points?"

Three separate things: the embedding is the middle one

⓪ Training: build the translator

Done once, before anyone uses it, with the matching game. Each round, the half-trained model also makes coordinates and gets corrected when they're wrong.
Who: model makers like OpenAI and Voyage

① Embedding: make coordinates

The trained translator turns a sentence (or an image, or a sound) into a list of numbers.
Who: the trained embedding model

② Use them: measure distance

Find the closest (search), recommend, de-duplicate, spot anomalies.
Who: vector databases and apps

Think of GPS: ⓪ is training a surveyor to draw accurate maps, ① is the surveyor giving every shop its coordinates, and ② is someone else using the map to find the nearest restaurant. Coordinates don't find you a restaurant by themselves, but once you have them, "what's nearby?" becomes simple arithmetic. When the support bot finds the right document, that's ① and ② together; ⓪ was finished long before.

Try it

Where the name comes from

  • Vector: the list of numbers itself.
  • Embed: placing a sentence onto the map of meaning.

The idea isn't new: in 2013, Google's word2vec put single words on a map of meaning. Today's models place whole sentences and documents.

It's not just text

  • Images: search by image, find similar products (a common model is CLIP)
  • Audio: turn the sound into a spectrogram, then into numbers; that's how "find similar songs" works
  • Products and users: recommendations, "people who bought this also bought…"

Footnote: the real list has over a thousand numbers (a thousand-plus dimensions). We draw it flat just to make it easy to see. And a single number on its own (say, 0.12) means nothing; the meaning comes only from which points sit near which.

4
Usage · how it's actually used

Three steps

① Prep

Turn every document, chunk by chunk, into a point, and store them.

② A question comes in

Turn the question into a point too.

③ Find the nearest

Hand the few closest chunks to the AI to answer.

Now the computer can search by meaning, however the customer phrases it. Recommending similar articles and spotting duplicates work the same way. So does spotting the odd one out: a point far from all the others might be a fraudulent transaction or a faulty machine. "Look up the material first, then answer" has a name in the industry: RAG (see the glossary below).

5
Do you build it yourself? · no, it's off the shelf

A ready-made "translator"

You don't have to build the text-to-numbers part yourself. Ready-made embedding models work like a translator: your app sends text through an API, and it sends the numbers back. OpenAI and Google both sell this, priced by the token (roughly, by the word). It's cheap.

What would it cost to turn all your docs into points?

Estimated at OpenAI text-embedding-3-small's price of $0.02 per million tokens; one-time cost to embed the docs.

The hard part · what makes it hard to build

Easy to grasp, hard to make top-tier

⓪ Training (building the translator, done once): it learns by playing a matching game: related pairs ("How do I get a refund?" ↔ "Refund policy") get pulled closer; pairs that look alike but aren't ("How do I get a refund?" ↔ "Return address") get pushed apart. Repeat that hundreds of millions of times.

So what is the translator itself? A trained neural network with its last layer removed (the layer that used to output an answer). Without it, the network outputs the list of numbers instead.

A neural network works like an assembly line: the first layers take in the raw input, the middle layers gradually "understand" it and turn it into a list of numbers describing its features, and the last layer turns that understanding into a specific answer, like "this is a cat" or "the next word is…".
Take an image model that tells cats from dogs. Used normally, it outputs "cat, 95%". With the last layer removed, it outputs [0.12, -0.83, …]: its impression of the photo (pointy ears, fur, whiskers…). Two different cat photos leave similar impressions, so they land close together on the map.
Why do it this way? "Cat or dog" only answers one question, but the impression from the layer before is general-purpose: good for comparing, searching and recommending. Today's text embedding models (like Qwen Embedding) work the same way: an LLM's last step is normally "predict the next word". Skip that step, take the numbers just before it, and that's the sentence's embedding.

FAQ: don't LLMs have an embedding too?

They do. An LLM's first step is to look each word piece up in a dictionary and get an ID (IDs carry no meaning), then its first layer, the embedding layer, turns IDs into meaningful numbers. That layer starts out random too and is trained along with the LLM. But it isn't the embedding model this page is about:

Take one real company apart

Voyage AI: training like a three-story building

Founded in 2023 by Stanford professor Tengyu Ma; acquired by MongoDB in 2025 (reported at about $220M).

First, what it sells: Voyage sells the translator, the part that makes coordinates (① embedding). The searching (②) happens in customers' own systems; Harvey, for example, puts Voyage's coordinates into its own database and searches case law there. The matching game in the three floors below is all ⓪ training.

↑ The higher the floor, the more specialized the model and the more specific the data
