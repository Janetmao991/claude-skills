## hook
h1 | Embeddings. What are they, really?
h2 | Let's start with a customer support bot.

## scene
s1 | Say you've built a support bot for your company.
s2 | The company has 500 documents: refund policy, shipping rules, membership info.
s3 | A customer asks: "I don't want what I bought. Can I get my money back?"
s4 | Before it can answer, the bot has to find the one document, out of 500, that has the answer.

## problem
p1 | The simplest way is to match the words.
p2 | But the customer said "money back," and the document says "refund."
p3 | Not a single word matches, so nothing comes up.
p4 | So the computer needs a new skill: telling whether two sentences mean the same thing.

## map
m1 | Computers don't understand meaning, but they're great with numbers.
m2 | An embedding turns each sentence into a list of numbers, like coordinates on a map.
n1 | That list of numbers is called a vector. Placing a sentence onto this map of meaning is called embedding it.
m3 | Sentences that mean similar things land close together: refund, money back, I want to return this.
m4 | "How long does shipping take?" lands far away, in another corner.
m5 | So "do these mean the same?" becomes "how close are these two points?"
m6 | In reality, the list has over a thousand numbers. We draw it flat just to make it easy to see.

## steps
k1 | So how is it actually used? Three steps.
k2 | Prep: turn every document, chunk by chunk, into a point, and store them.
k3 | When a customer asks something, turn the question into a point too.
k4 | Find the nearest: grab the few chunks closest to it, and hand them to the AI to answer.

## api
a1 | You don't have to build the text-to-numbers part yourself.
a2 | There are ready-made embedding models. Think of one as a translator.
a3 | Your app sends text through an API, and it sends the numbers back.
a4 | OpenAI and Google both sell this, priced by the token. It's cheap.

## goal
g1 | And now the computer can search by meaning.
g2 | However the customer phrases it, the bot finds the right document.
g3 | Recommending similar articles and spotting duplicate content work the same way.

## biz
b1 | So where does this sit in the real business world? Picture a production line.
b2 | Upstream are the companies selling the translator: OpenAI, Google, Cohere, and in China, Alibaba, Zhipu, Baidu and ByteDance.
b3 | There are also free, open-source models like BGE and Qwen Embedding that you can download and run yourself.
b4 | A million tokens costs about two cents. It's a volume business.
b5 | Midstream are the warehouses: vector databases like Pinecone and Milvus. Veterans like MongoDB and Postgres have added it too.
b6 | Database companies are even buying model makers: MongoDB bought Voyage AI, and Elastic bought Jina AI.
b7 | Downstream are the buyers who build it into products: Shopify's search bar, Notion AI, Intercom's support bot, GitHub Copilot.
b8 | Because when search fails, users leave and orders disappear.
b9 | And at the end of the line is you: plain words find the product, and the support bot finally gets what you mean.

## moat
t1 | So how hard is it to build this translator?
t2 | The idea is easy to grasp, but getting to the top tier is hard.
t3 | It learns by playing a matching game: pull related pairs closer, and push away pairs that look alike but aren't.
t4 | Repeat that hundreds of millions of times, and it learns.
t5 | These pairs are mostly collected by machines or generated by AI, not labeled by hand one by one.
t6 | Microsoft's E5 used about 270 million pairs. Alibaba's Qwen Embedding generated about 150 million.

## tiers
y1 | The real barriers come down to four things: data, compute, a base model, and benchmark scores.
z1 | Base model means good translators are usually adapted from an existing large language model, so companies that already have one start ahead.
z2 | Benchmark scores are public rankings, like a standardized exam: MTEB worldwide, C-MTEB for Chinese. Customers check this leaderboard first.
y2 | So here's the landscape: big labs use massive data to build general models. That's very hard to do.
y3 | Specialists build industry models, like Voyage's models for law, finance and code.
y4 | Everyone else uses an off-the-shelf model, or fine-tunes an open-source one on their own data.

## voyage
v1 | Let's take one company apart: Voyage AI, founded in 2023 by Stanford professor Tengyu Ma, and later acquired by MongoDB.
v2 | Its training stacks up like a building with three floors.
v3 | Floor one: train a general translator that knows a little about everything.
v4 | Floor two: on top of that, it learned about a trillion more tokens of legal text, and played the matching game with purpose-built pairs. That made the law model.
v5 | Finance and tech data were mixed in too, so it wouldn't get lopsided.
v6 | Across eight legal retrieval tests, it beat OpenAI's large model by six percent on average.
v7 | Floor three: a custom model for the legal AI company Harvey. First it read over twenty billion tokens of US case law,
v8 | then it was refined on questions and cases annotated by Harvey's lawyers.
v9 | The result: nearly a quarter less irrelevant material at the top of the results, with vectors a third the size.
v10 | General, industry, customer. The higher the floor, the more specialized the model, and the closer the data gets to the customer.

## recap
x1 | So remember it in one line: an embedding is the part that finds things by meaning.
x2 | Model makers sell it, databases store it, and software companies build it into their products.
x3 | You use it every day, in search bars and support bots. Save this one!
