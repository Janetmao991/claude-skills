# Part I — The YC circle

## Paul Graham — Founder-first: determination + relentless resourcefulness; company-level test: growth
Source: https://paulgraham.com/founders.html ("What We Look for in Founders", essay) · https://paulgraham.com/relres.html ("Relentlessly Resourceful", essay) · https://paulgraham.com/growth.html ("Startup = Growth", essay) — all fetched and verified
Core lens:
- Above a threshold of intelligence, determination matters most — but paired with flexibility: like a running back, "determined to get downfield," willing to go sideways or backwards moment to moment
- The one-sentence founder test: are they "relentlessly resourceful" (the opposite of hapless)? Startup obstacles are novel and external, so founders must keep trying new things rather than plow through
- Look for imagination (good ideas seem bad at first) and "naughtiness" — delight in breaking rules that don't matter — plus genuine friendship between cofounders
- A startup is defined by one thing: designed to grow fast. Benchmark: 5–7% weekly growth is good, 10% exceptional, 1% means the model isn't figured out; revenue (or active users pre-revenue) is the metric
Use when: judging early founders with little traction, and deciding whether something is a real startup vs. a nice small business.

## Sam Altman — Startup Playbook: great product, desperate users, momentum
Source: https://playbook.samaltman.com/ (essay/handbook, verified) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CBYhVcO4WgI (Stanford CS183B Lecture 1, video, verified; full course at startupclass.samaltman.com)
Core lens:
- The idea must make target users say they desperately need it, and be explainable in one sentence — "complex ideas are almost always a sign of muddled thinking"; prefer new-and-hard over derivative-and-easy
- Founder traits: "unstoppability, determination, formidability, and resourcefulness"; a fast proxy is being unusually responsive
- "Have a great product. This is the only thing all great companies have in common" — founders must talk to users directly, recruit the first ones manually, tight feedback loop
- Momentum is the prime directive: one growth metric the company obsesses over, retention tracked alongside acquisition, no vanity metrics
Use when: evaluating a seed-stage company end-to-end (idea clarity, founder quality, product loop) or judging what a pre-PMF team should prove next.

## Kevin Hale — "How to Evaluate Startup Ideas": idea = hypothesis (problem × solution × insight)
Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DOtCl5PU8F0 (YC Startup School 2019 lecture, video, verified)
Core lens:
- A startup idea is a hypothesis for why the company will grow fast: Problem × Solution × Insight (unfair advantage)
- Good problems are popular, growing, urgent, expensive to solve, mandatory, and frequent — want at least one, ideally several
- Never start from the solution ("SISP" — solution in search of a problem)
- The insight must map to an unfair advantage in one of five buckets: founders (top-10-in-world expertise), market (growing ~20%/yr), product (10x better), acquisition (free/organic channels), or monopoly (network effects)
- Distinguish threshold beliefs from miracle beliefs — a good bet needs only one miracle
Use when: scoring a pre-traction idea/pitch — the most systematic YC rubric for "is this fundable before there's data."

## Dalton Caldwell — Tarpit ideas and structural skepticism about "validated" demand
Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MvZumqI93K4 ("Avoid These Tempting Startup Ideas", YC video with Michael Seibel, verified) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m7LvNTbaqSI (Lenny's Podcast, "Lessons from 1,000+ YC startups", verified)
Core lens:
- Beware "tarpit ideas": consumer ideas everyone likes and validates — abundant positive feedback, a graveyard of failed attempts, users who never actually engage
- If a problem has existed unsolved for years, assume a structural reason previous founders failed — research the body count before believing
- Team first: technical excellence plus founder-market fit (built related tools or lived the problem professionally)
- To judge market size, look for existing large companies doing something adjacent, rather than trusting TAM slides
Use when: stress-testing an idea that "everyone loves" — especially consumer/social — and checking whether founders have a right to win.

## Garry Tan — Earnestness and backing misfits before the market believes
Source: https://fs.blog/knowledge-project-podcast/garry-tan/ (The Knowledge Project #226, podcast — page verified, full transcript members-only) · https://www.hustlefund.vc/post/angel-squad-garry-tan-investments-what-y-combinators-president-teaches-about-betting-on-the-earliest-believers (secondary write-up, verified)
Core lens:
- The rarest and most predictive founder quality is earnestness — genuinely caring about the problem and users — not polish or pitch theatrics (don't "LARP" being a founder)
- Fund founders, not ideas: YC's formula is selecting people it can transform, at ~1% acceptance
- Back founders before anyone else believes (the $150K Coinbase check) — founders "the market hasn't priced correctly"
- Beyond the person: strong early traction, product-market pull, team visibly driven by mission
Use when: judging whether a founding team is the real thing — especially unpolished, contrarian, or "misfit" founders credential filters would reject.

## Paul Buchheit — "If your product is Great, it doesn't need to be Good" + judge founders, not ideas
Source: https://paulbuchheit.blogspot.com/2010/02/if-your-product-is-great-it-doesnt-need.html (essay, verified) · https://capitalandgrowth.org/paul-buchheit/ (interview on 200+ angel investments, verified)
Core lens:
- "Pick three key attributes or features, get those very, very right, and forget about everything else" — the iPod's three: portability, storage, Mac sync; ~80% of effort into the three pillars
- If a product needs everything to be acceptable, it's incremental, not innovative
- After 200+ investments: "investing purely on the quality of the idea is inversely correlated with success" — you must meet and judge the founders
- Red flags: idea sourced from reading TechCrunch rather than lived experience; two years of work with nothing shipped; obstacle-finders rather than action-takers
Use when: judging a product's design focus (v1 products) and separating founder quality from idea quality at angel stage.

# Part II — Big-firm GPs

## Marc Andreessen / a16z — Product/Market Fit ("The Only Thing That Matters")
Source: https://pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html (essay, 2007, archived from his original blog — fetched and content-verified)
Core lens:
- Of team/product/market, market matters most: "Rachleff's Law" — when a strong market meets a weak team, the market wins; a killer team in a terrible market still loses. A great market "pulls product out of the startup."
- PMF defined operationally: "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market" — the dividing line between pre-success and post-success startups.
- You can feel its absence: word of mouth not spreading, usage growing slowly, long sales cycles, deals not closing; and its presence: customers buying as fast as you can ship.
- Pre-PMF, do "whatever is required" to get there — rewrite the product, change markets, replace people; nothing else matters until then.
Use when: judging early-stage startups where the key uncertainty is demand — is this a real market pulling the product, or a team pushing one?

## Bill Gurley / Benchmark — Revenue Quality & Marketplace Physics
Source: https://abovethecrowd.com/2011/05/24/all-revenue-is-not-created-equal-the-keys-to-the-10x-revenue-club/ (essay, 2011) and https://abovethecrowd.com/2012/11/13/all-markets-are-not-created-equal-10-factors-to-consider-when-evaluating-digital-marketplaces/ (essay, 2012). Site blocks automated fetches (403); content verified through mirrors/summaries.
Core lens:
- A dollar of revenue is not fungible: only businesses with sustainable competitive advantage deserve high revenue multiples — score revenue quality, not size.
- The 10X-club checklist: network effects, visibility/predictability (recurring vs episodic), lock-in/switching costs, high gross margins, marginal profitability at scale, low customer concentration, no partner dependence, organic demand growth.
- For marketplaces: new-experience delta vs incumbent, economic advantage to both sides, tech value-add, fragmentation of supply and demand (high = good), transaction friction, payment flow-through (rake feasibility), network-effect potential.
- Beware "growth at low marginal economics" — a business that must buy every incremental customer has low-quality revenue no matter how fast the top line grows.
Use when: evaluating marketplaces, SaaS, or any revenue-stage company where the question is "does this growth deserve its valuation?" — unit economics and moat, not narrative.

## Peter Thiel / Founders Fund — The Seven Questions & Monopoly Theory
Source: Zero to One (2014 book, final chapter "Seven Questions"; summary: https://davidcummings.org/2024/11/16/thiels-seven-questions-every-business-must-answer/) + "Competition is for Losers" Stanford CS183B Lecture 5 (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5_0dVHMpJlo)
Core lens:
- Seven questions: Engineering (10x better, not incremental), Timing (why now), Monopoly (big share of a small market first), People, Distribution (a real channel, not just a product), Durability (defensible in 10–20 years), Secret (a truth others don't see). A company needs good answers to 6 of 7.
- Aim for monopoly, avoid competition: competitive markets destroy all profits; a valuable company must capture some of the value it creates.
- Start in a deliberately small market you can dominate, then expand concentrically (PayPal → eBay power sellers first).
- Last-mover advantage: most of a tech company's value lies in cash flows 10+ years out, so durability beats early growth rate.
Use when: judging contrarian/deep-tech or category-creating bets — "what do you believe that nobody else believes, and can it become a monopoly?"

## Roelof Botha / Sequoia — Founder-Problem Fit, Pre-Mortems & Pre-Parades
Source: Tim Ferriss Show #618 transcript, https://tim.blog/2022/09/01/roelof-botha-transcript/ (interview; 403 to automated fetch, verified via excerpts) + Invest Like the Best EP.250 (https://www.joincolossus.com/episodes/66371765/botha-sequoias-crucible-moment). Concrete artifact: his 2005 YouTube investment memo (https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/sequoias-investment-memo-on-youtube/37221597).
Core lens:
- Founder-problem fit: "It starts with an authentic identification with a problem… if you're doing it for the wrong reasons, you're going to wilt" — authentic obsession is the top founder signal.
- Ambition alignment: Sequoia screens for "outliers who want to build really, really big businesses" and checks founder ambition against the return math.
- Every memo includes a pre-parade (imagine wild success 3–5 years out — what had to go right?) and a pre-mortem (what killed it?) — forcing explicit why-now and failure-mode reasoning.
- Decade-plus horizon: "the majority of the value accrues after the IPO" — weight long compounding over exit timing.
Use when: seed/Series A founder judgment — is this person authentically fused to the problem, and does the timing story survive a written pre-mortem?

## Vinod Khosla — Gene Pool Engineering (team = risk management)
Source: https://www.khoslaventures.com/posts/gene-pool-engineering-for-entrepreneurs (essay, 2015 — fetched and verified) + companion talk (https://www.khoslaventures.com/gene-pool-engineering-cycle-times-risk-vinod-khosla, video)
Core lens:
- "A company becomes the people it hires" — evaluate the team-to-risk mapping, not the team in the abstract.
- The method: identify the 5 mission-critical risks; for each, find organizations that already solved it and the top practitioners there; each hire should retire a named risk.
- Startups are exercises in risk elimination — far more actionable than "hire great people."
- Deliberately mix veterans (lessons learned) with young outsiders (fresh eyes); repeat the exercise for upside opportunities, not just risks.
Use when: deep-tech/science bets with heavy technical risk — does the team's composition actually match the risks that could kill the company?

## Keith Rabois / Founders Fund — Spikes, Barrels & Ammunition
Source: "How to Operate," Stanford CS183B Lecture 14 (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6fQHLK1aIBs) + talent heuristics summary (https://foundersjournalpod.morningbrew.com/blog/how-to-identify-top-1-talent-keith-rabois-investor-in-doordash-youtube-airbnb-lyft/) + Village Global "Identifying Talent and Evaluating Markets" (podcast)
Core lens:
- Look for a spike, not well-roundedness: top-1% excellence on one dimension, a rare Venn combination of skills, or the ability to navigate the idea maze from zero to the big prize.
- 10x founders = a spike plus tenacity: "over a wall, under a wall, through a wall, making friends with the wall."
- Barrels vs ammunition: barrels take an idea and make it happen end-to-end; a company's parallel throughput = number of barrels (~5–10% of the org).
- Guard against quality degradation as you scale: hire undiscovered talent before the market prices it; use multiple data points, not one interview.
Use when: judging founders and early teams pre-traction — is there a genuine spike + relentlessness, and can the founder be a barrel who ships?

# Part III — Angels and the AI era

## Naval Ravikant — "How to Angel Invest" (Spearhead doctrine)
Source: https://nav.al/angel-1 and https://nav.al/angel-2 (podcast with full transcripts, with Babak Nivi; existence confirmed via search — direct fetch blocked by Cloudflare); content cross-verified via https://www.navalmanack.com/secret-sections/investing (essay/compilation, fetched OK) and https://spearhead.co/angel (podcast index)
Core lens:
- Buffett's three-part partner test applied to founders: high intelligence, high energy, high integrity — missing integrity gives you "a hard-working, smart crook"; intelligence is judged by whether they've thought about the problem unusually deeply (specific knowledge), not years of experience
- Energy red flags: hesitation, needing constant positive feedback, being easily diverted — startups are "the Olympics of business"
- Portfolio math over stock-picking: see ~10,000 deals, pick the few with a shot at being huge, expect power-law returns from 1–2; his own audit of ~300 investments and ~15 unicorns found no repeatable thesis beyond founder quality plus staying inside his knowledge edge (consumer mobile/social, 2010–2014)
- Personal filter before writing a check: do you like them, can they teach you something, and are the economics good
- Judgment compounds through brand: bad picks cost you deal flow, not just money — reputation is the angel's real asset
Use when: seed/angel-stage bets where there's no data yet and the decision is almost entirely a read on the founding team.

## Elad Gil — "AI Market Clarity" and the GPT-capability-ladder lens
Source: https://blog.eladgil.com/p/ai-market-clarity (essay, fetched and verified); companion context in https://blog.eladgil.com/p/early-days-of-ai (essay) and High Growth Handbook (book, https://growth.eladgil.com/ — free full text; note the book is about scaling, not screening)
Core lens:
- Markets "crystallize" in waves: he tracks which AI verticals have suddenly produced a clear 1–2 leader in the past 12 months (legal, code, foundation models) vs. which are still open — and invests against the crystallization timing, not the abstract TAM
- "GPT ladder": each model-capability threshold unlocks whole markets that previously didn't work (legal failed on GPT-3.5, took off on GPT-4) — so ask "what does the next model level unlock, and who is positioned pre-fidelity to capture it"
- Building early against customers before models are good enough is a feature: you capture share the moment the models catch up
- Business-model shift test: the winners sell "units of cognition / human-equivalent labor," not seats — look for revenue trajectories like $0→$500M in 2 years in code tools
- Distinguish technology problems from organization/process/people problems — the latter don't get solved by a better model, and incumbent lock-in strength decides whether a startup can win at all
Use when: deciding whether an AI vertical is investable *now* — market-timing and market-structure questions more than founder-quality questions.

## Mike Maples Jr. (Floodgate) — Pattern Breakers: inflections × insights × founder-future fit
Source: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-find-a-great-startup-idea-mike-maples-jr (podcast with transcript, fetched and verified); https://patternbreakers.substack.com/p/how-inflections-let-startups-change (essay); book: *Pattern Breakers* (2024)
Core lens:
- Inflection test: is there a concrete external shift (tech, regulatory, behavioral) that changes what people *can* do — not just a trend narrative; startups win by escaping comparison, not by being "better"
- Insight test: the founder's core belief must be both non-consensus AND right — an "earned secret" from hands-on immersion; his litmus question to founders is "what future are you living in?" and the best answer with lived experience, not slides
- Backcasting: stand in the envisioned future and reason back to the present, rather than extrapolating the present forward
- Polarization as signal: seek products people love or hate at first contact; lukewarm neutral reactions rarely become breakouts — find "lighthouse customers" with desperate, specific need
- Founder-future fit: the founder's skills, motivation, and network must match the specific future they're claiming — a great founder for the wrong future still fails
Use when: pre-seed/seed ideas that look weird or "too early" — separating genuinely non-consensus bets from merely contrarian-and-wrong ones.

## Sarah Guo (Conviction) — "Software 3.0" conviction investing
Source: https://colossus.com/article/sarah-guo-conviction/ ("Sarah Guo's Wager," essay profile — link confirmed via search, direct fetch blocked); criteria cross-verified via https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-sarah-guo/ (sourced compilation, fetched OK) and https://freeplay.ai/blog/insights-from-cutting-edge-ai-product-teams-a-conversation-with-sarah-guo-founder-of-conviction (interview); her podcast No Priors (podcast, weekly, most episodes transcribed)
Core lens:
- Concentrated conviction over spray-and-pray: ~27 investments in 3 years, few boards — she commits to "a person and an overall idea," suspends disbelief, and works the company for years; the diligence bar is correspondingly personal and deep
- End-user-value test for AI: "if you don't get it all the way to end user value... it doesn't matter" — lab capability without deployed workflow value is disqualifying
- "Software 3.0" bar: winning teams need traditional software rigor *plus* novel model-native product work, and must keep up with capabilities that change monthly (prompting/context strategies going stale is a real diligence check)
- Founder profile: technical depth plus "a personal vendetta" — obsession with shipping and results, composure under adversity ("not panicking"), speed, and an "undeniably good product"
- As code-writing costs collapse, she weights product instinct, taste, and clarity of intent more heavily than raw engineering capacity
Use when: early-stage AI-native startups where the question is "is this a real deployed product with a relentless founder, or a capabilities demo."

## Aileen Lee (Cowboy Ventures) — the Unicorn Club dataset
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2013/11/02/welcome-to-the-unicorn-club/ (essay, fetched and verified); 10-year update: https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/18/welcome-back-to-the-unicorn-club-10-years-later/ (essay)
Core lens:
- Base rate first: only 39 US unicorns existed in 2013 (~0.07% of funded startups) — any evaluation must start from how rare the outcome is, not the pitch narrative
- Experienced, co-history teams win: average founder age 34, ~80% had founded before, 90% of cofounding teams shared years of school/work history (work history outperformed school ties), average 3 cofounders — the "young solo dropout" is the exception, not the pattern
- Original vision holds: ~90% of unicorns were still on their founding product vision — big successful pivots are outliers, so weight the initial thesis heavily
- Enterprise quietly outperforms: enterprise unicorns returned ~26x their private capital vs. much lower for consumer, even though consumer companies dominated headlines and count
- Network effects appeared in nearly half the cohort; geography concentrated (27/39 Bay Area) — and she flagged the diversity gap (zero female founding CEOs) as both a finding and an opportunity
Use when: sanity-checking pattern-match claims about teams and outcomes against actual base rates — especially seed-stage team composition questions.

## Martin Casado (a16z) — the economics of AI companies (with Matt Bornstein)
Pick justification: most-cited AI-infrastructure GP with a written-down, unit-economics-first evaluation framework for AI startups.
Source: https://a16z.com/the-new-business-of-ai-and-how-its-different-from-traditional-software/ (essay, fetched and verified); companions "The Empty Promise of Data Moats" and "AI's costs" essays on a16z.com
Core lens:
- Gross-margin reality check: AI companies run 50–60% margins vs. 60–80%+ for SaaS, from inference compute plus 10–15% of revenue on humans-in-the-loop and data labeling — model the variable costs, don't hand-wave them
- Long-tail-of-intent test: 40–50% of intended functionality can live in the edge cases; each new customer brings a novel data distribution, so ask whether one model serves many customers or every deal needs bespoke fine-tuning (the latter scales like services, not software)
- Data moats are weaker than pitched: architectures are published, pre-trained models are open, network effects in data are "relatively weak" and can hit diseconomies of scale — defensibility must come from the product around the model, not the model itself
- Prefer narrow problem domains (bounded data complexity) and business plans whose economics work with *today's* models, not ones that require assumed future tech advances
Use when: diligence on AI infra/applied-AI companies where the question is "does this scale like software or like a consulting firm" — margin structure, COGS, and moat claims.

# Part IV — Moat theory and report craft (added 2026-10-02)

## Hamilton Helmer — 7 Powers (the competition scorecard grid)
Pick justification: the only moat taxonomy precise enough to score cell-by-cell; adopted by Acquired as the backbone of every episode's analysis section, which is why it reads as the industry's shared vocabulary.
Source: *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy* (book, 2016); summary at https://7powers.com/ ; applied weekly on Acquired (acquired.fm — "Power" segment of every episode)
Core lens:
- A Power = a condition that creates persistent differential returns AND that competitors cannot easily copy. Both halves must hold — benefit without barrier is just a feature
- The seven: **Scale economies** (unit cost falls with volume), **Network effects** (value rises with users), **Counter-positioning** (newcomer adopts a model the incumbent can't copy without hurting itself), **Switching costs** (customer loses value by leaving), **Branding** (durable willingness to pay from identity/trust), **Cornered resource** (preferential access to a scarce asset — talent, data, license), **Process power** (embedded org capability others can't replicate quickly)
- Stage mapping: counter-positioning and cornered resource are the Powers available at *origination*; scale, network, switching costs come at *takeoff*; branding and process power only at *stability*. A seed company claiming brand or process power is almost always wrong
- Most AI-app companies today score "building" at best on switching costs and "none" on everything else — that is a legitimate finding, not a scoring failure
Use when: filling the §4 competition scorecard (subject + top 2 rivals, every row, has/building/none + evidence) and deciding whether "differentiation" claims in the deck are a Power or a feature.

## Acquired (Ben Gilbert & David Rosenthal) — Playbook · Power · Bull/Bear · Grading
Source: https://www.acquired.fm/ (podcast; every episode ends with the same four-part analysis ritual)
Core lens:
- Separate *history* (what happened) from *analysis* (why it worked) — the analysis only starts once the facts are fully laid out, never interleaved
- **Playbook**: the 3–5 repeatable moves that explain the outcome (a distribution hack, a pricing choice, a wedge-then-expand sequence) — asked as "what could another founder copy"
- **Power**: run the Helmer grid explicitly, naming which Powers the company has *now* vs. is *building*
- **Bull / Bear**: written as two honest advocates, not as a bull case with caveats; then a letter grade with the one-sentence reason
Use when: structuring §4 (playbook read) and §9 (bull/bear two-column) so the bear case has a real author.

## 海外独角兽 / 拾象 — thesis-first company deep dives (Chinese-language benchmark)
Source: WeChat `unicornobserver` (not web-searchable); mirrors: https://foresightnews.pro/column/detail/101 · https://www.chaincatcher.com/specialt/52 . Verified samples 2026-10-02: Granola (2025-06), Mercor (2025-06), Pylon (2025-04)
Core lens:
- Open with **投资逻辑**: 3–4 bets plus the main risk, before any evidence — the report is a memo, not a profile
- **Why now + player taxonomy**: explain why the category is reopened by AI *at this moment*, classify the players into 3–4 kinds, and place the company among them; TAM numbers are supporting cast
- One defining product choice (Granola: AI augments the human's notes rather than replacing them) carries the product section; feature lists do not
- Data density of an investor, not a journalist: retention cohorts, dilution per round, named customer scorecards (Mercor: Google rated it 6–7/10 vs Scale 8–9/10)
- Weak spot to compensate for: structure drifts per article (the Pylon piece has no risk section) and technical depth is shallow — hence pairing with the Contrary skeleton
Use when: writing §0 and §3 of the report; and as a checklist for whether a Chinese article is analysis (has 投资逻辑 + risks) or a rewritten founder interview.
